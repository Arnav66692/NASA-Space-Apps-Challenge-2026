Blue Ghost watched the Sun go down.

Firefly's Blue Ghost landed on March 2, 2025 and worked through the lunar day. Replay the original scenario at the Blue Ghost site and watch the modeled Sun cross the smooth horizon on March 16.

Two days earlier, Earth covered the Sun.

On March 14, 2025 Earth passed in front of the Sun as seen from the Moon. On Earth it was a total lunar eclipse. The model puts the Sun fully behind Earth at the Blue Ghost site from 06:18 to 08:35 UTC. The reported totality for the whole Moon was 06:26 to 07:31 UTC, and the model window covers it. Methods & sources

Why it matters

Most landers run on sunlight and talk to Earth by direct radio, so the hours with the Sun and Earth up are the hours a mission can work. Intuitive Machines' Athena landed near Mons Mouton on March 6, 2025, on its side in a cold crater where its solar panels could not recharge it. Its batteries ran down and the mission ended about 13 hours after landing. NASA's October 2024 list of nine Artemis III candidate regions includes Mons Mouton and Mons Mouton Plateau. Solar landers are usually built for one lunar day of about 14 Earth days. Blue Ghost stopped after sunset on March 16, 2025, and Chandrayaan-3 never woke after its first night in 2023. What the numbers mean

Sun above horizon marks a geometric opportunity for solar power. Earth above horizon marks a geometric opportunity for direct-to-Earth radio. A body counts as above when the centre of its disk is above the smooth reference horizon (elevation > 0°). On polar terrain, nearby ridges can materially change both windows. When Earth fully covers the Sun (a lunar eclipse seen from the surface), that time is counted as no sunlight. Partial cover is marked on the timeline and still counted as sunlight. The terrain margin raises the line that counts as the horizon to 1, 2 or 5 degrees, as a stand-in for ridges and crater rims. It is the same in every direction around the site, so it is a what-if, not a measured horizon. The LOLA terrain horizon replaces the flat line with the skyline seen from 2 m above the ground at each built-in site, traced out to 150 km over NASA's LOLA elevation map (CGI Moon Kit, 16 pixels per degree, Moon curvature included). That map has about 1.9 km pixels north to south, so near the poles small ridges and crater rims are smoothed and the skyline is an estimate. Custom points use the smooth horizon. The mission need is a number of hours with the Sun and Earth both above the horizon, without a break. A stay meets it when its longest unbroken stretch is at least the need. The go and no go marks, the site table and the backup finder all use this same test. NASA: shadows near the south pole ↗

Far-side relay. Earth does not rise on the far side, so a lander there talks to Earth through a relay satellite. The relay panel uses an illustrative orbit, because the exact orbits are not public. Chang'e 4 uses Queqiao, in a halo orbit around the Earth-Moon L2 point about 65,000 km from the Moon. The model puts Queqiao on a 13,000 km circle around the point opposite Earth, with a 14 day period. The size, the period and the phase are assumptions. Chang'e 6 and custom far-side points use Queqiao-2, in a 24 hour frozen orbit of 200 km by 16,000 km, inclined 55 degrees, with the closest point over the north. The orbit plane is fixed to the stars, and its node and phase are assumptions. Other sources give a different measured orbit. The relay counts as in view when it is above the smooth horizon, checked every 10 minutes. The model assumes that the relay always sees Earth. Data

The opening view is four hypothetical touchdowns at two real landing sites, each 73 hourly samples (72 intervals) from the JPL Horizons API ↗: observer tables from surface site coord@301, geodetic coordinates at zero altitude, Sun (10) and Earth (399) centres, apparent airless azimuth/elevation (QUANTITIES=4), UTC. Azimuth runs clockwise from north. Site coordinates from NASA NSSDCA ↗. The CSVs ship with the site and the collector script (Get-LunarHorizons.ps1) regenerates them. Raw JPL files: Athena original, Athena delayed, Blue Ghost original, Blue Ghost delayed.

Every other site, date and slip is computed in the browser from one bundled table. It holds the points on the Moon where the Sun and Earth are overhead, hourly from 2024 through 2030, from the same JPL Horizons service (Moon seen from Earth's centre, quantities 14, 15, 19 and 20). The app turns those points into azimuth and elevation at the chosen site on a smooth sphere at zero altitude, then runs the same calculation as the presets. No live request is made.

Calculation

Crossings are linearly interpolated between hourly samples (t = t₀ + e₀/(e₀−e₁)·Δt). Above-horizon intervals are merged, summed, and intersected for “both above”. The bars, totals, countdowns and sky views all read from the same intervals. The change is delayed − original, rounded only for display. Between samples the sky view interpolates elevation linearly and azimuth along the shorter arc.

Deep Space Network link. Earth above the site horizon is not enough. A DSN antenna on Earth must also see the Moon. The app computes the Moon's apparent elevation from the 70 m antennas at Goldstone (DSS-14), Madrid (DSS-63) and Canberra (DSS-43) every 5 minutes. It uses the lunar series in Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, chapter 47, with nutation and sidereal time. A complex counts when the Moon is 10° or more above its horizon. Link hours are the hours with Earth up at the site and at least one complex on the Moon. The elevations agree with JPL Horizons within 0.004° at 30 test times in March 2025.

Checks completed

Automated tests reproduce every reference total and crossing for all four presets (wholly above, wholly below, rising, setting, overlap). Blue Ghost's sunset was bracketed with a separate five-minute JPL query. The table method was checked against direct JPL queries at 10 sites and 10 dates. The bar was 0.5° and was set before the check ran. All 200 Sun and Earth directions agree within 0.006°. The table also reproduces all 584 Sun and Earth directions in the four preset files within 0.006°. For the eclipse of March 14, 2025 the model puts the Sun behind Earth at the Blue Ghost site from 06:18 to 08:35 UTC, which covers the reported totality of 06:26 to 07:31 UTC. The slip strip uses the same calculation as the table, and its cells match the four preset files within 0.2 hours. Lander-tilt validation is out of scope, and a test with first-time users has not been run yet.

3D views

The globe is the real Moon. Its surface is NASA's LRO Camera colour mosaic, and its relief comes from LOLA laser altimeter elevation, both from the CGI Moon Kit by NASA's Scientific Visualization Studio ↗. Relief is drawn about three times steeper than it is, so it reads at this scale. The globe converts each site's local Sun and Earth directions to Moon-fixed vectors to light the surface and to draw the sunrise/sunset line and the Earth-horizon line (Earth parallax across the Moon, ≤0.3°, is ignored in the globe only). The opening screen is the same globe seen from its night side, exposed brighter.

The surface view is an original procedural rendering, not imagery. It places the Sun and Earth by azimuth and elevation. The lander is a generic model. Sizes and terrain bumps are illustrative.
